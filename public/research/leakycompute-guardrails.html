LeakyCompute: measuring exposed AI without becoming the threat

Internet-exposed AI services are easy to count badly and surprisingly hard to study responsibly. I built LeakyCompute to measure the problem without turning the measurement system into another scanner, proxy, or source of false confidence.

Mahdi Hedhli

TLDR: LeakyCompute is a research project that passively catalogs exposed AI endpoints using minimally intrusive verification. It keeps passive index counts, an archive snapshot, and bounded historical re-verification separate, and reports reachability—not demonstrated impact. After an adversarial Daybreak Blue review found serious flaws in identity, data handling, target validation, and probe scheduling, I suspended active discovery and hosted checks. On 27 August 2026, I shipped cryptographically verified researcher identity, durable probe leases and cooldowns, one-time permits, canonical public-address validation, fresh-source requirements, last-moment exclusion checks, conservative rate limits, and tightly constrained probes with pinned destinations, fixed request shapes, no redirects, hard timeouts, and capped responses. Those controls passed a production canary; scanning resumes only while the strong control plane is healthy.
Operational note — 27 August 2026: Scheduled discovery is temporarily paused because the control store reached its daily write allowance. Permit issuance fails closed, so no target traffic can leave while the strong store is unavailable. The public dashboard is serving clearly marked, last-known aggregate counters until the store recovers; scanning will resume only after a packet-free health check and another capped canary pass.

The question was never just "how many?"

The project began after I saw a short-lived public catalog of exposed AI infrastructure. The raw number was arresting, but the more useful question was what that number actually meant. Was a host merely visible in a search index? Had it answered a recent request? Did an endpoint expose metadata, inference, a workflow engine, or a path to code execution? Those are different claims, and collapsing them into one counter makes the research less useful.

So LeakyCompute now presents three measurements side by side. As of 27 August 2026, an archive-era catalog listed 19,348 hosts. Public-index queries returned 19,376 matching records; because query lanes overlap, that is not a distinct-host count. The rolling 180-day re-verification corpus retained 1,351 distinct hosts that answered a read-only request. These figures are not additive. Each has a different source, time boundary, and confidence level.

The current index count is entirely Shodan-derived, so source bias is not a footnote. It is part of the result. Tunnelled services are also invisible to this method. The live project page keeps the timestamp and source split next to the numbers so they cannot quietly turn into a timeless claim.

Rules before features

I wrote the safety rules as versioned invariants before treating the project as a measurement platform. The important ones are deliberately unglamorous: use only reviewed metadata, health, version, or listing requests; send GET requests only; never follow redirects; cap every response body; contact only reviewed ports; honor exclusions before any request; and report what was reachable without proving what an attacker could do with it.

The system is split across three trust boundaries. The public site receives only aggregates. Approved researchers can reach a minimised corpus through the lab. Administrative ingest and authorization remain behind a third boundary. Keeping those surfaces separate limits what a bug in any one of them can expose.

That design still surfaced meaningful classes of exposure. As of 27 August 2026, the rolling 180-day retained corpus contained 960 host–stack pairs where models or inference were reachable, 188 host–stack pairs where unauthenticated workflow execution was reachable, 129 host–stack pairs where training metadata or artifacts were readable, and 78 host–stack pairs where Jupyter or Ray exposed the prerequisites for code execution. These are host–stack pairs, not distinct hosts. A host can expose several stacks and appear in more than one class, while a re-verified host may fall into none of them. The four classes therefore overlap, are not exhaustive at the host level, and should not be summed to the 1,351 distinct hosts above. These are reachability classifications. I did not submit jobs, run notebooks, pull models, send prompts, or otherwise demonstrate impact on third-party systems.

The 1,351 figure is an as-of count from a rolling corpus, not a permanently frozen snapshot. Resumed verification adds or refreshes qualifying observations in that corpus, while remediation and retention can cause other records to leave it.

Discovery and verification remain separate operations. Scheduled discovery never walks address space or synthesizes neighboring targets; candidates must be nominated by fresh records from approved public indexes—currently Shodan, with Censys planned. Operator-owned hosted checks use a separate authorization path and never become discovery provenance.

Red teaming the research system with Daybreak Blue

Once the project had a public Worker, a gated lab, local runners, scheduled workflows, and several ways to ingest observations, reading the happy path was not enough. I used Daybreak Blue as an adversarial reviewer alongside a primary code review pass. The review covered the Worker, public clients, lab, Pages bridge, runners, storage model, workflows, Cloudflare configuration, CLI, and tests. It did not probe a third-party target.

Daybreak's most useful habit was to treat every safety claim as something to falsify. If the system said a researcher was approved, could a different Access user manufacture the trusted identity? If a target parser rejected private addresses, what happened with an integer-encoded address or a hostname that changed after validation? If a rate gate recorded a probe after it finished, what happened when the process crashed in the middle? Those questions found problems that ordinary feature testing did not.

Identity and data boundaries

  1. Researcher impersonation. Any valid Access user could supply a trusted-looking login header, or collide through an email local part or mutable display claim, and impersonate an allowlisted researcher. Production identity now comes only from the exact email in a cryptographically verified Access application token. Issuer, audience, token type, lifetime, algorithm, signature, and key ID are checked.
  2. Public workflow artifacts. Scheduled discovery wrote raw candidate addresses to downloadable artifacts and logs in a public repository. The uploads were removed, output was redacted, and 13 artifact and workflow log sets were deleted. No credential leak was confirmed, but deletion cannot revoke copies that were already downloaded or cached.
  3. Alias lifecycle errors. Research aliases could survive some revocation and reapproval sequences, and one researcher could collide with another researcher's alias. Ownership conflicts now fail before a write, removed aliases are deactivated, and alias matches re-check the active primary identity.
  4. Bridge route confusion. Path normalization in the lab bridge could turn a researcher route into an administrative one. The bridge now uses an exact route inventory, a header allowlist, manual redirects, and no-store responses.
  5. Identity stored in denial records. Denied lab requests stored raw signed email addresses, while some lab scans performed excessive key-value reads. Denial identity is now hashed, scans are capped, and per-user rate limits apply.

Target validation and probe safety

  1. False clean results from the hosted checker. The Worker platform's global fetch API could not reliably reach IP-literal targets. A failed connection could therefore look like a clean host. Target traffic now crosses a dedicated address-pinned TCP socket boundary inside the API Worker. It accepts only a consumed control-plane permit for one canonical IP, reviewed service, port, and request path—with DNS resolution and redirects disabled—and reports connection or platform failures as indeterminate, never clean.
  2. Address parser bypasses. Reserved, documentation, benchmark, and multicast ranges were incomplete. Numeric address encodings, hostnames, DNS rebinding, and CIDR opt-out edges created additional bypasses. Targets now pass through one canonical parser, only public-unicast IP literals are accepted, and hostnames are rejected.
  3. Probe ledger timing. The attempt clock was written after a run. A crash could repeat a probe, and ledger eviction could weaken cadence. The clock now moves through a transactional lease taken before traffic leaves the runner, and a one-time permit means an interrupted run cannot re-authorize itself.
  4. Ungated legacy discovery. An older runner could probe arbitrary seeds and neighboring addresses without the newer provenance, exclusion, interval, and rate gates. That runner is retired from production scheduling and hard-fails any non-dry CLI run before target traffic. The guard is a runtime check rather than removed capability; the code path exists and is gated, not deleted. It remains only for passive reporting and packet-free planning. Scheduled discovery now runs exclusively through the control-plane-gated path.
  5. Amplification through malformed input. Duplicate services, inherited object keys, and truthy authorization values could expand a request beyond its intended work. Inputs are now exact, deduplicated, bounded, limited to owned keys, and require the literal boolean value true.
  6. Stale provenance. An old index record could make a now-private or reserved target appear eligible, or preserve standing permission indefinitely. Public-unicast validation now happens first and index provenance expires after 7 days.
  7. A costly health check. One LiteLLM health route can trigger paid upstream completions. The profile now uses its liveness endpoint, which checks service health without spending the operator's API budget.
  8. Redirect and response-size gaps. Off-Worker helpers followed redirects, some reads were unbounded, the local Ollama client lacked a response cap, and favicon loading could cross origin. Redirects are blocked across runners, reads are capped at 32 KiB, non-loopback local checks need attestation, and icons stay same-origin.

State, reconciliation, and automation

  1. Partial aggregates presented as complete. A reconciliation job could count only the newest 2,000 records and overwrite complete totals. Recounts now checkpoint a fixed corpus snapshot and publish only after a complete pass.
  2. Reusable exclusions. An exclusion approval could be reused after an issue was edited, and an incomplete purge could be reported as complete. Approvals are now bound to the label event and body, and deletion status reports partial work honestly.
  3. Unsafe workflow parsing. One approval flow read the wrong event field and constructed JSON through shell interpolation. It now reads the correct event data and uses a structured JSON builder.
  4. Enumerable abuse pseudonyms. When a secret was missing, abuse logs fell back to a public salt. They now use HMAC-SHA256, and production skips the pseudonym rather than weakening it.
  5. Fail-open administrative ingest. Exclusion lookup failures and caller-supplied replacement sets could weaken the effective block list. Administrative ingest now fails closed and unions server-side exclusions.
  6. Client-side trust expansion. An arbitrary ?api= parameter could redirect the browser client to another endpoint. Disclosure timing could also be misconfigured, browser headers were too weak, and exception strings could reveal internals. The override is gone, timing is validated, response headers are stricter, and public errors are bounded.
  7. Supply-chain drift. Floating dependencies, actions, and container images made the reviewed build different from a future build. Dependencies and actions are pinned, images use immutable digests, and CI checks those constraints.

What had to exist before probing came back

Fixing the concrete bugs above was not enough. Each one was covered by a test, but tests on individual defects do not make an architecture ready for unattended internet measurement, so I treated four structural gaps as blocking.

A durable pre-probe ledger had to reserve work before traffic left the runner, so a crash mid-probe could not produce a repeat. Corpus storage needed authoritative pagination rather than eventually consistent key-value scans. Opt-out deletion needed a resumable, verifiable completion record. Retention and full reconciliation had to work across the whole corpus without silently degrading into a partial pass.

What shipped was a strongly consistent control plane with transactional leases and one-time probe permits, authoritative cursor-paged corpus storage, resumable opt-out jobs with durable completion records, and checkpointed retention and reconciliation jobs that publish new aggregates only after a complete pass. Production verification meant interrupting and resuming each workflow across a multi-page corpus, confirming that no second probe authorization was issued, late-page opt-outs and expired records were removed, partial reconciliations never became public, and only one tightly constrained request reached an operator-owned canary.

With those in place, scheduled discovery and hosted checks can run only under the controls listed above. The production canary passed, and any loss of the strong control plane stops new permits rather than weakening those controls. The project reads public index aggregates, serves the historical research corpus to approved researchers, and lets operators run the local checker against infrastructure they own.

The scope is narrow by design, and the narrowness is the point.

What I learned

The hardest part of defensive measurement is not writing the probe. It is making every surrounding system keep the same promise. Identity, logs, retries, storage limits, redirects, workflow events, and dependency pins all become part of the safety boundary. A read-only request can still cause harm if it reaches the wrong address, repeats after a crash, spends someone else's API budget, or leaves a sensitive trail in public automation logs.

Daybreak Blue changed the project by forcing those promises into adversarial questions and executable tests. The result is not a claim that LeakyCompute is finished or risk-free. It is a clearer record of what the system supports today, the controls governing those capabilities, and what remains deliberately out of scope.

What's next?

More independent data sources

The current index view is entirely Shodan-derived. Censys is the next source I want to add, but the goal is not to manufacture a larger total. It is to compare source-specific counts, overlap, disagreement, freshness, and blind spots. Censys records will stay labeled and separate, and any publication will follow the access tier and research terms attached to the account. The project's own probes will not fill gaps in a passive source.

Certificate Transparency and other passive signals may follow. Hostnames are more identifying than an address in a large hosting network, so retention, lab visibility, deletion, and publication rules need to be settled before those records are stored. Until then, new sources remain counts and candidates, not standing permission to contact a host.

Responsible disclosure through an established partner

Measurement matters only if it helps the right operator fix the exposure. The next external step is to approach The Shadowserver Foundation with methodology and aggregate context, but no host-identifying data. I want to learn whether it would rather add the reviewed fingerprints to its own scanning and reporting pipeline or evaluate a minimised observation feed through a private channel it specifies.

There will be no improvised uploader. Any handoff needs an accepted schema, secure transport, provenance and freshness rules, acknowledgement and retry behavior, and a way to propagate opt-outs and corrections. Raw records derived from a third-party index will not be forwarded without the permission required by that source. If Shadowserver is not the right route, national CSIRTs, CERT/CC, and provider abuse channels remain the documented fallbacks.
